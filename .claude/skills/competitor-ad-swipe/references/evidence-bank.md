# Evidence bank — what we are allowed to claim, and from what

Source of truth: the Notion **Clinical Studies Tracker**
(`collection://566126a9-95e2-47e5-a170-ff00c52fb1d7`). Re-read it before citing anything; this
file was synced 2026-09-28 and the tracker moves.

"No approved social proof" has blocked four briefs. It is not that we have no evidence — it is
that most of it cannot carry the weight an ad puts on it. This file says which is which.

## The one study that can carry an ad

**Hool & Carpenter (2025) — VeRelief Prime taVNS for acute anxiety.** The only study in the
library that is *device-only*, *sham-controlled*, and run on an *anxious population*. Everything
else fails at least one of those three.

- Randomized, single-blind, sham-controlled. n=24 (15 active / 9 sham).
- 10 minutes of stimulation at the tympanomastoid site — behind and below the ear, which is
  where the product is actually placed.
- **Relaxation: 100% active vs 33% sham (p=0.001).** This is the headline. Use this one.
- Responders at or above the clinically meaningful threshold: 67% active vs 33% sham.
- Anxiety scale (STAI): −24% active vs −20% sham.
- Safety: no serious adverse events; minor symptoms similar in both arms.

> **The trap.** The sham group also dropped ~20% on the anxiety scale. So "the real one worked
> and the fake one didn't" is **false** and must never be written. The separation is in the
> relaxation rate and the responder rate, not the anxiety scale. Anyone tightening a script into
> "the placebo did nothing" has introduced a claim the study does not support.

Caveats to keep visible when it matters: n is small, participants self-diagnosed rather than
clinician-diagnosed, the sham was 0 mA rather than sensory-matched, and the document is an
**internal, AI-assisted commercialization writeup marked CONFIDENTIAL — not peer-reviewed.**
Putting its figures in a public ad is a decision for Mark each time, not an inherited default.

In-script phrasing that clears the compliance gate, in plain language, no jargon:

> It's one small study — twenty-four people. But some got the real device, some got a fake one,
> and nobody knew which. Ten minutes in, nearly everyone with the real one said they felt
> relaxed. About a third of the fake group did.

**Lead with the limitation, and round the number down.** The true figure is 100%, and 100% is
the least believable number in advertising — stating it accurately makes the honest claim sound
invented. "Nearly everyone" understates an approved figure, which is always compliant, and it is
the version a viewer believes. Naming the small n *first* buys the rest of the sentence: a claim
that concedes its own weakness reads as evidence, a claim that doesn't reads as a pitch.

Same logic in the visuals: a designed stat card or animated bar chart reads as advertising.
Show the actual document — title page, a slow scroll over the results — plain and unstyled.

Never "clinically proven", never a treat/cure/prevent frame, never an FDA indication.

## Supporting, but not for ads

**Hool (2020) — bilateral ABVN+GAN at tympanomastoid, golfers.** Sham-controlled (0 mA), state
anxiety ~41→26, significant. Same stimulation site as Prime, so it independently corroborates
the mechanism — but the population is golfers, so it is internal backup, not ad copy.

**Hool (2020) PhD dissertation.** The parent work. Safe bilateral taVNS raised HRV measures;
tympanomastoid lowered state anxiety. Cite as "peer-reviewed-adjacent" background only.

## Do not cite for efficacy

**Tyler, Wyckoff, Hearn & Hool (2019), n=120.** Establishes that the dry-hydrogel earbud
electrodes are safe and tolerable — genuinely useful for safety questions. But **autonomic
effects were often shared with sham.** That is a null-ish result, and it is the reason we do not
make HRV, heart-rate or other physiological claims in ads.

**Recharge Room case studies — Gilbert teachers (2023) and first responders (2026).** The
biggest numbers in the library (99.7% positive, ~90-95% less stressed, >96% pain/inflammation)
and the most dangerous. Both are **device plus other recovery modalities**, with **no sham**,
on self-report. Nothing in them can be attributed to VeRelief alone. An ad saying "95% felt less
stressed" from these is unsupportable. They are fine for B2B and Recharge Room decks, where the
stack *is* the product.

> **Adoption is not efficacy, and adoption is usable.** The Recharge Room programme still yields
> a claim an ad can carry, because counting installations says nothing about whether the device
> works: *"Trusted by 20,000+ professionals and 33+ Recharge Room locations across six states"*
> (Mark, 2026-09-28 — **get the underlying count on file; it is not in the tracker**). Scale and
> institutional adoption are strong proof for a skeptical buyer and carry none of the
> efficacy exposure. The line to never cross is a *performance* number from these programmes.
> Adoption claims still need substantiation like any other objective claim, so the source for
> the 20,000 and the 33 belongs in this file before it runs again.

**Morning VNS & outbound sales field study (2025), n=20.** No sham, performance metrics, healthy
adults. Fine for a B2B or performance angle; irrelevant to panic and not evidence of calm.

## The rule

Device-only claim → Hool & Carpenter, and only the relaxation or responder figure.
Safety question → Tyler 2019.
Everything else → no number. Qualitative language, or nothing.
