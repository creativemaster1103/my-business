## Comment-response ad fuel

A comment-response ad uses a real comment as the hook. The comment does the whole stop-the-scroll job in the first second, and the creator answers it directly, the way people already talk on Reels and Shorts. The test is simple. If a stranger scrolling past would read it out loud to whoever is next to them, it is a candidate. If it reads like a review, it is not.

Two things make Hoolest unusually well supplied here. First, the brand has a live, loud, hostile comment section. The ad comment surface holds roughly 5,981 rows, and 50 of them contain the word scam, 58 mention TENS units, and 31 mention placebo. That is real tension sitting in public, already written in native platform voice. Second, the review corpus contains confessions and disbelief lines that read like comments rather than reviews.

The single highest-leverage tactic for this brand is the reversal. Take the top objection, put it on screen as the comment, and let the response be the customer who used to believe it. The account has both halves already written by real people.

This is the most-liked skeptic comment in the whole surface, which is the closest thing to a vote the platform gives you. It works as a hook because it is specific about money, specific about the alternative, and delivered with total confidence. The viewer who has had the same thought sees themselves. ## Personas

Seven types show up in the reviews. Three of them match the ICP set in the Parker brand context document. Three are not named there at all. One is named there and barely appears.

A caution before the profiles, straight from the persona method. Confidence scales with the evidence tier, and the strongest tier is missing entirely. Post-purchase surveys hold zero responses for this brand, verified by direct query. Everything below is built on reviews and comments, which sit two rungs down. There is no purchase data, no age, no gender, no region on any row. So every demographic read here is inferred from how the person writes, never verified, and it must not harden into a fact downstream.

One structured field the corpus does carry is the product title, and bucketing by SKU is a mining-method requirement, so here is the real split across all 139 rows, counted row by row. The Prime family takes 69 of 139, made up of 45 for the Lasting Relief Bundle, 17 for the Prime Gen 2, and 7 for the plain Prime. The Mini takes 39. The headphone products take 12, split 6 for the VeRelief Pro and 6 for the Hoolest Pro. Consumables and accessories take 11 across the Activator Gels, the Replacement Gel Tip Subscription, the 30 Day Supply tips, and the Small Electrodes. Hoolest Sleep takes 5. MiniMax GO and Route Package Protection take 1 each. Verified from the structured field. Two things follow. The Prime is where the review weight sits, which matches its status as the focus product. And 11 of 139 reviews, 7.9 percent, are people reviewing a consumable rather than a device, which is why the gel tip objection is louder in this corpus than its underlying frequency would suggest.

## Open loops

**Loop 1 — The clinician channel nobody named.**

Practitioners keep showing up in the reviews as buyers who then hand the device to other people. Doctor, clinic, patient, client, chiropractor, counselor, and therapist language runs through 18 of 139 reviews, 12.9 percent, at an average of 4.44, and one reviewer asked outright whether wholesale pricing exists. None of the five ICPs in the brand context names this person.

Pull: **Gap.** There is a whole segment writing reviews, and no persona, no creative, and no offer has ever been built for it.

Question: How much of the brand's revenue and referral traffic actually comes through practitioners?

Why it is a loop: If a meaningful share of buyers arrive through a chiropractor, a counselor, or a clinic, the fastest growth lever is a practitioner offer rather than another cold-traffic angle, and the creative brief changes from persuading a sufferer to equipping a professional.

Territory: Personas.

**Loop 2 — Sleep outranks panic in the reviews, and the positioning leads with panic.**

Sleep and night language reaches 41 of 139 reviews, the biggest theme in the corpus, at an average score of 4.88. Anxiety and panic language reaches 37 of 139. The brand context says the positioning gap the brand owns is the panic-ready crisis tool, territory no competitor has claimed. The reviews are not disagreeing with that so much as pointing somewhere else.

Pull: **Tension.** The brand's stated position and the customers' stated reason to reach for the device are not the same thing, and both are well evidenced.

Question: Which door brings in more first-time cold buyers, sleep or panic?

Why it is a loop: The answer decides the lead angle for every top-of-funnel ad, and the two doors need different creative, different proof, and different compliance handling.

Territory: Messaging.

**Loop 3 — The people rating the shipping instead of the product.**

Seventeen of the 29 negative reviews are about an order that never arrived, and the review request flow is firing on undelivered orders, which generates more of them. Twenty-two of 139 reviews carry shipping language at an average of 1.86.

Pull: **Surprise.** A 4.11 average looked like a healthy product score until the rows were read one at a time and most of the bad ones turned out to be about a box.

Question: What is the real product satisfaction rate once undelivered orders are separated out?

Why it is a loop: Every downstream decision that reads the star average as a quality signal is reading a fulfillment signal instead, and the fix might be a flow change rather than a product change.

Territory: Product.

**Loop 4 — The device the buyer never bought because they never got an answer.**

The ad comment surface holds roughly 5,981 rows. Fifty-eight mention TENS units, 50 mention scams, 31 mention placebo, and 98 mention gel tips, mostly as the same question asked over and over. The brand answers nearly all of them, one reply at a time, in the comments. None of those answers appears in an ad.

Pull: **Pattern.** The same handful of questions keeps reappearing across independent reels and posts, month after month.

Question: How many of these objections would stop recurring if the answer moved from the reply box into the creative itself?

Why it is a loop: If pre-purchase objections are being handled one comment at a time, the brand is paying for reach and then hand-answering the friction. Moving the answer into the ad is the cheapest scale lever available.

Territory: Messaging.

**Loop 5 — The first responders who are not in the reviews.**

The brand's most developed ICP is Marcos, the fire captain, and the brand context leads with 1,000-plus first responders as a trust marker. In 139 reviews there are roughly two people who identify that way, one combat veteran and one surgical nurse.

Pull: **Gap.** The persona the brand has invested the most thinking in leaves almost no trace in the one customer surface Parker can read.

Question: Where do first responders who buy this device actually talk about it?

Why it is a loop: If they buy and stay silent, the brand needs a different listening surface to learn anything about them. If they are not buying at scale, the trust marker is doing reputational work rather than describing a real segment, and the creative built for Marcos is aimed at a smaller room than anyone thinks.

Territory: Personas.

**Loop 6 — The chronic-illness door.**

Migraine, Long Covid, Lyme, Crohn's, seizures, vasovagal episodes, and chronic throat clearing appear across 17 of 139 reviews at an average of 4.41. The brand correctly declines every one of these in its comment replies. Customers keep arriving through the door anyway.

Pull: **Curiosity.** People are finding this product through search terms and doctor conversations that have nothing to do with stress, and the brand can see it happening without being able to speak to it.

Question: How are chronic-illness buyers finding this product in the first place?

Why it is a loop: The discovery path matters even when the message cannot follow it. If these buyers arrive through a doctor, that reinforces the practitioner loop. If they arrive through a forum or a search, that is a channel the brand has never mapped.

Territory: Product.

**Loop 7 — Who is on screen, and whether it matches who writes the reviews.**

This audit reads reviews, not creative, so it cannot see the ads. But the persona set surfacing here includes a counselor, a chiropractor, a mother of an eight-year-old, a woman with twenty-one years of ME/CFS, a nurse in surgery, and a golfer. Whether any of those people appear in the brand's creative is unknown from this seat.

Pull: **Gap.** A whole cast of people is writing about this product, and this audit cannot tell whether any of them has ever been cast in it.

Question: Which of the customer types writing these reviews has never appeared on screen in the brand's creative?

Why it is a loop: The brand says founder-led is what wins and also says its creative bottleneck is angles. If the review corpus is full of people who have never been cast, that is a supply of angles sitting unused.

Territory: Creators and talent.

**Marked for the brand.** Loop 1 and Loop 3 can only be answered with data the brand holds. The practitioner share needs order data or a survey. The true satisfaction rate needs the fulfillment records joined to the review rows.
