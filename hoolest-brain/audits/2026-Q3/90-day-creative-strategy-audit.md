# 90-day creative strategy audit — Hoolest Performance Technologies — 2026-Q3

## Executive summary

The account is running one story, told by three people, at enormous volume. Over the trailing ninety days Hoolest spent 1,045,684.92 dollars across 923 active ads and bought 6,920 purchases at a 1.33 blended ROAS and a 151.11 dollar CPA. Verified from `search_facebook_ads_sql`, window 2026-06-10 to 2026-09-08. Of those 923 ads, 879 were built and launched inside the window itself. That is roughly 290 new ad entities a month, and it is the single loudest fact about this account. The brand told us its ambition is 50-plus net-new concepts a month. The account is running at nearly six times that in ad units. What it is not doing at the same rate is finding new things to say.

The structural pattern is a narrow message spine funded very wide. One desire, freedom from fear, pain and danger, carries 400 of the 568 tagged ads and 660,508.79 dollars, 63.2 percent of all window spend. One emotion, Hope, sits on 278 tagged ads and 632,718.71 dollars, 60.5 percent of spend. Three human faces, the founder, one functional neurologist and one whitelisted creator, carry 72.2 percent of top-ten spend. The top ten ads are 1.08 percent of the account by count and 39.24 percent by spend, and they return a blended 1.34 ROAS. That last number is the quiet alarm. The ads Meta chose to fund hardest are no more efficient than the account average. Spend concentration is not buying performance concentration.

The strongest creative bet inside the top-spend set is the comparison lane, and it is badly underfunded. HPT025, the founder walking through five rival devices by name, returned a 1.82 ROAS on 35,222.59 dollars at a 101.51 dollar CPA, the best economics of any ad in the top ten. Across the whole window the Comparison format is nine ads and 73,581.82 dollars, 7.0 percent of spend, and it posts a 1.46 ROAS and a 126.21 dollar CPA. Both beat every larger format in the account. Authority Figure, Educational and Founder Ads together absorb 782 thousand dollars and return 1.30, 1.24 and 1.24. The account's biggest lanes are its least efficient lanes, and its most efficient lane is the one it barely runs.

The most consequential movement since last quarter is emotional and it went the wrong way. Anxiety went from 5.8 percent of spend to 22.9 percent, and Relief went from 4.9 percent to 18.2 percent. The account leaned hard into agitation. But Relief converts better than Anxiety on both measures that matter, a 1.35 ROAS against 1.26 and a 142.65 dollar CPA against 153.63, and it still carries 50 thousand dollars less. Meanwhile Overwhelm, which held 9.8 percent of spend last quarter across six ads, collapsed to 1.2 percent this quarter even as the team built 49 ads carrying it. Meta funded eight times more Overwhelm creative into one-eighth the budget. The account is being told something about that emotion and has not read the message yet.

The surprise sits between the ads and the reviews. The paid creative sells panic. Panic appears in the hook, the headline, the price card and the product name across nearly every top-spend ad. In the 139 reviews written in this same ninety-day window, panic appears in 8 of them, 5.8 percent. Sleep appears in 38, 27.3 percent, nearly five times as often, and those sleep reviews average 4.87 stars against a 4.11 corpus average. The other half of that gap is the SKU. Reviews name the VeRelief Mini 39 times and the VeRelief Prime 17 times, while the paid account sells the Prime almost exclusively. The ads are pitching a crisis product to a customer base that keeps describing a nightly ritual.

The throughline the big-picture section resolves is this. Hoolest has built an enormous, disciplined creative machine on top of one message, aimed it at the sharpest and rarest moment in the customer's life, and funded it with an escalating discount ladder that now runs four different prices at once inside the same account. That combination is why volume tripled, CPA fell 30.6 percent, and AOV still fell 25.4 percent, leaving ROAS to inch from 1.24 to 1.33 on 42.5 percent more spend. The machine is working. The message is the constraint.

## Deep dive on each of the top 10

### 1. Prime _ PDP_BOFU _ Product-in-Hand _IMG _ V1 — 95,711.38 dollars, 1.33 ROAS, 170.30 CPA, 562 purchases, 1.70 percent CTR

The single largest line of spend in the account is a still image. On a flat light-gray field, a heavy black sans-serif headline reads BACK IN STOCK, and under it in a lighter weight, "Resets your nervous system instantly." The center of the frame is a close, dramatically lit photograph of a Black man's hand, palm up and slightly curled, cradling a small matte black device. The lighting is harsh enough to throw deep shadow into the palm and pick out the ridges of the fingers, which makes a pocket gadget read as medical hardware. The device carries an embossed H, a row of five white status lights and two square buttons marked minus and plus. To the right of the hand, 399 dollars sits struck through in black and 199 dollars sits under it in hot red, with the line "Try VERELIEF PRIME." beneath. A footer strip carries "RECOMMENDED BY 1,000+ CLINICIANS" in thin white type and five gold stars, the last one half filled, next to "4.7/5 STARS." No face, no body, no scene. One hand, one price, one badge. Verified from the Parker creative summary and image asset.

The lever pulling the weight here is scarcity dressed as demand, and it is doing the work of a message the ad never actually makes. BACK IN STOCK is not a benefit claim, it is a social proof claim in disguise. It tells the reader other people bought this until it ran out, which is the one argument a skeptical wellness buyer cannot argue with. The AI tag set reads the ad as FOMO plus Hope and as Product Aware, which is right. This ad is not persuading anybody that vagus nerve stimulation works. It is closing somebody who already half believed it and needed a reason to move today. Verified from the ad's tag record and its own copy.

Read against the account it looks less impressive than its spend suggests. This ad alone is 9.2 percent of all window spend and its 1.33 ROAS is exactly the account blended average, to the second decimal. Its CPA of 170.30 dollars is 12.7 percent worse than the account's 151.11. Its outbound CTR is 0.66 percent, the lowest in the top ten and the only one under the 1 percent floor by a wide margin, which the own-account reading method treats as the sign that the call to action is not earning belief. What saves it is basket size. Its 226.11 dollar AOV is the second highest in the top ten and 12.3 percent above the account's 201.29. That is the price ladder working. People who arrive through this ad buy more per order, which is what you would expect from a bottom-of-funnel offer card, not from a creative that changed anyone's mind. Inferred, confidence strong.

The funnel-stage caveat matters more here than anywhere else in this audit. This is a bottom-of-funnel demand-capture static competing on a leaderboard against top-of-funnel video, and the account reading method is explicit that those are not the same job. Meta gave it the most money because it converts warm traffic cheaply in the auction's terms, not because it is the account's best creative. Judging it as the account's number-one ad would be the exact misread the breakdown effect is there to warn against. Inferred, confidence strong.

What it implies for the next round is uncomfortable. The strongest thing in this ad is the hand shot, and the account has never built a top-spend video around the same visual idea, a real hand holding a real device under hard light with nothing else in frame. The second strongest thing is the clinician badge, and no ad in the top ten casts an actual clinician other than the one neurologist. The third thing is the strike-through, and that is where the trouble starts, because this static anchors at 399 while three other live ads in the same window anchor at 400 and one anchors at 199. More on that in the big picture.

### 2. HPT027_VID_Founder Story _2B — 51,222.85 dollars, 1.19 ROAS, 164.70 CPA, 311 purchases, 40.41 percent hook, 8.74 percent hold

Cut to an indoor room where the same man now wears a navy Verelief polo. "I had bad performance anxiety and nothing worked to calm me down," he says. "So I got my PhD in biomedical engineering so I could create something so I wouldn't have to be dependent on medications my whole life." He lifts the device toward the lens, shows two clear gel tips, and offers the line the whole account leans on: "It kind of looks like a taser." An anatomical head-and-neck diagram lights up the auricular branch of the vagus nerve and traces it to the brainstem while he explains the top-down calming effect in about thirty seconds. The lever is credentialed vulnerability, and it is the account's most durable asset. He tells you he was broken before he tells you he is qualified, which is the sequence that disarms the "another gadget" reflex the brand context names as a core objection. The hook rate of 40.41 percent clears the 30 percent bar the account reading method sets and clears it well. Verified.

Then the ad falls apart in the middle. The hold rate is 8.74 percent, well under the 12 percent floor, and the drop-off series shows why: 58.8 percent of viewers who reach the quarter mark are gone by the halfway mark. Average play time is 6.2 seconds on a 79-second film. The outbound CTR is 0.93 percent, under the 1 percent floor. This is the classic strong-hook, weak-body pattern the iteration doctrine names, and the diagnosis is not mysterious. Between second 28 and second 65 the ad stacks a research chart, a refund dashboard, a 700,000 dollar figure, a price drop, a gel subscription explainer with two monthly price points and a competitor teardown. That is five proof arguments and two pricing structures in under forty seconds. The killer-ads bar calls this out directly: if people are confused they will not buy. Inferred, confidence strong.

The most instructive thing about this ad is not the ad, it is its sibling. HPT027_VID_Founder Story _1B launched the same day, into the same campaign and the same ad set, with the same script family and a different edit. It posted a 49.98 percent hook rate, an 18.97 percent hold rate, a 1.77 ROAS and a 110.25 dollar CPA on 10,583.93 dollars. The version with a hold rate more than twice as good and a ROAS 49 percent better received one-fifth the budget. The breakdown effect explains why that is not a mistake in Meta's terms, and the honest read is at the ad-set level rather than the ad level. But the creative lesson survives the caveat cleanly: the same story, cut tighter, holds more than twice as many people. Verified on the numbers, inferred on the cause, confidence mixed because the ad set may hold ads this pull did not surface.

## Desires — Life Force 8

Drew Whitman's Life Force 8 names the eight biological wants advertising can actually reach: survival and enjoyment of life and life extension; enjoyment of food and beverages; freedom from fear, pain and danger; sexual companionship; comfortable living conditions; to be superior, winning and keeping up with others; care and protection of loved ones; and social approval. Parker's tag layer classifies against that list directly, so this section reads the account's own tags rather than reclassifying by hand. The denominator is again 568 tagged ads and 945,280.96 dollars. Verified.

The account is a single-desire account. Freedom from fear, pain and danger carries 400 of the 568 tagged ads, 70.4 percent by count, and 660,508.79 dollars, 63.2 percent of all window spend and 69.9 percent of tagged spend. Comfortable living conditions is second at 36 ads and 118,795.54 dollars, 11.4 percent. To be superior, winning and keeping up with others is third at 19 ads and 86,053.20 dollars, 8.2 percent. That concentration is defensible on its face. A product sold to stop a panic response is a freedom-from-fear product, and pretending otherwise would be worse strategy than leaning in. But the efficiency data undercuts the size of the bet. Freedom from fear returns a 1.30 ROAS at a 149.95 dollar CPA, both slightly worse than the account's 1.33 and 151.11. Verified. The account's dominant desire is its worst-returning desire, and the two it barely touches both beat it.

Read what those two desires actually are in this account and the picture sharpens. Comfortable living conditions is almost entirely the comparison lane. ## Stage of awareness

Eugene Schwartz's five stages run from a person who does not know they have a problem to a person who knows your product and only needs an offer. Parker's tag layer classifies against all five. The denominator is 568 tagged ads carrying 945,280.96 dollars, and the tagged-count distribution and the spend distribution disagree sharply, which is the most useful thing in this section. Verified.

By count, the account is a problem-aware account. Problem Aware is 283 of the 568 tagged ads, 49.8 percent. Most Aware is 142 ads, 25.0 percent. Solution Aware is 117 ads, 20.6 percent. Product Aware is 24 ads, 4.2 percent. Unaware is 2 ads, 0.35 percent. By spend, the ranking flips at the top. Solution Aware takes 388,135.11 dollars, 37.1 percent of all window spend. Problem Aware takes 344,320.13 dollars, 32.9 percent. Product Aware takes 130,088.15 dollars, 12.4 percent. Most Aware takes 82,026.53 dollars, 7.8 percent. Unaware takes 711.04 dollars, seven-hundredths of one percent. Verified.

Hold those two lists next to each other and the shape of the account appears. One hundred and forty-two Most Aware ads share 82,026.53 dollars, an average of 578 dollars each. Twenty-four Product Aware ads share 130,088.15 dollars, an average of 5,420 dollars each, nine times as much per ad. One hundred and seventeen Solution Aware ads share 388,135.11 dollars, an average of 3,318 dollars each. The account produces offer creative in bulk and funds explanation creative in depth. Inferred, confidence strong.

Efficiency by stage is unambiguous and it points the same way the format and desire sections did. Solution Aware returns a 1.43 ROAS at a 141.91 dollar CPA and a 77.58 dollar cost per lead, the best of any stage on all three. Product Aware returns 1.41 at 156.92. Most Aware returns 1.39 at 150.78. And Problem Aware, the second-largest spend bucket in the account, returns 1.23 at a 158.02 dollar CPA, the worst stage on both. Verified. Solution Aware beats Problem Aware by 16.3 percent on return and 10.2 percent on cost per purchase.

That matters enormously because of where the quarter's new money went. Last quarter Problem Aware was 44 ads and 82,000.80 dollars, 11.2 percent of window spend. This quarter it is 283 ads and 344,320.13 dollars, 32.9 percent. The account added 21.7 points of spend share and 262,319 dollars to its least efficient awareness stage. Meanwhile Solution Aware, the best-returning stage, sat almost perfectly still, moving from 37.0 percent to 37.1 percent of spend while growing from 76 ads to 117. Product Aware went from 3.0 percent to 12.4 percent, quadrupling. Most Aware went from 27 ads and 105,754.81 dollars, 14.4 percent, to 142 ads and 82,026.53 dollars, 7.8 percent, which is five times the ads for 22 percent less money. Verified.

The default-starting-point doctrine in the performance-ads material is worth holding against that. It says the strongest place to open an account is solution aware with a problem-solution lead, because it reaches a wide pool that is still warm enough to convert, and those ads have the best odds of turning evergreen. This account has that lane, it is the account's best-returning lane, and the account chose to grow around it rather than into it. Inferred, confidence strong.

The Unaware end is effectively empty. Two ads and 711.04 dollars across a quarter in which the brand spent over a million. The brand's stated business objective is scale acquisition, and its own context says brand awareness is the place it loses hardest to Pulsetto, which it describes as significantly out-marketing it. The material on brand size is direct that at Hoolest's spend level, roughly 350,000 dollars a month, the plays that matter are founder ads, consistent volume, and reaching audiences beyond the people already inclined to buy. The account is doing the first two very well and has run 711 dollars of the third. That is the classic amplification-mode profile applied by a brand whose own diagnosis says it is still in a foundation fight for awareness. Verified on the spend, inferred on the read, confidence strong.

There is a real counter-argument and it deserves airtime. Unaware creative is expensive, slow to attribute, and hardest to judge without any attribution beyond Meta's own, which is exactly this account's situation. With post-purchase surveys dark and no third-party attribution connected, an unaware-stage test would be very difficult to read honestly, and the team may have chosen not to spend into a measurement blind spot. That is a defensible call. It is also a reason the measurement gap is more expensive than it looks. Data-limited.

The drag this imbalance creates shows up in three numbers that moved together. Across the quarter CTR fell from 5.62 percent to 2.23 percent, a 60.3 percent decline. Cost per link click rose from 1.21 dollars to 2.94, up 143 percent. And CPM rose modestly from 30.58 to 32.50. Verified. Cheaper impressions, far more expensive clicks. That combination reads as an account reaching more people less relevantly, which is what happens when volume triples at the problem-aware stage without a corresponding widening of who the problem creative is for. Inferred, confidence mixed, because the prior window's 5.62 percent CTR is itself unusual and is inflated by the BulbFX creative's 4.94 percent at 98,547.55 dollars.

The mix does at least match one part of the business stage honestly. A brand fighting for its first real scale should be heavy in the middle three stages, and 82.4 percent of this account's spend sits in Problem, Solution and Product Aware combined. The composition inside that block is the problem, not the block itself. Inferred, confidence strong.
