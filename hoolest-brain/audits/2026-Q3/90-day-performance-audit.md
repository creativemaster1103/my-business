# 90-day performance and delivery audit — Hoolest Performance Technologies — 2026-Q3

## Executive summary

The account got much bigger and much more efficient at the same time, which almost never happens together. Over the 90 days from 2026-06-10 to 2026-09-08 the Hoolest FB Ads account spent $1,045,684.92 and returned $1,392,894.14 in Meta-reported purchase value, a 1.33 ROAS on 6,920 purchases. Against the trailing 90 days of 2026-03-12 to 2026-06-09, spend is up 42.5 percent from $733,915.93, purchases are up 105.3 percent from 3,371, and cost per purchase fell 30.6 percent from $217.71 to $151.11. That is verified from the Meta pull. The engine behind it is visible in the account structure: 923 individual ads carried spend this window against 377 last window, a 144.8 percent jump in the number of distinct ads live. Read through Andromeda v2, that is the creative-diversity play working as designed, more genuinely different ads reaching more separate pockets of audience rather than more copies of the same thing.

The largest shift in spend distribution is by platform. Instagram took 55.2 percent of spend at $577,716.75, up from 43.5 percent and $319,509.09 last window, while Facebook fell from 54.8 percent to 44.3 percent. In dollars Facebook did not shrink, it grew 15.2 percent, but Instagram grew 80.8 percent and took the lead. That move is inferred to follow the HPT creative system, which started shipping into the account in mid-June. The first HPT ads in this window were built on 2026-06-17 and the run continued through 2026-08-07, and they are vertical, mobile, founder-voiced video. Meta pushed them where that shape lives. The dominant audience segment by spend is now 35 to 44 at 34 percent and $355,450.92, and the account's center of gravity slid younger across the board: every bracket 44 and under grew faster than the account's own 42.5 percent spend growth, and every bracket 55 and up shrank in real dollars.

Two things sit outside a healthy range and both deserve the reader's attention. First, the quarter's trend inside itself is down, not up. Broken into months, ROAS runs 1.44 in June 10 to 30, 1.38 in July, 1.27 in August, and 1.15 in the first eight days of September, while CPM climbs from $32.39 to $29.59 to $34.36 to $43.81. Verified from four separate windowed pulls that sum exactly to the quarter total. The account is buying more expensive impressions each month and converting them worse. Second, hook rate is falling. Across the sixteen top-spending video ad groups carrying $429,395.18, or 54.9 percent of this window's video spend, the spend-weighted hook rate is 34.5 percent and the hold rate is 10.8 percent. Last window's fourteen top video groups, carrying $315,988.88 or 65.4 percent of that window's video spend, ran a 47.4 percent weighted hook rate and an 11.6 percent hold. Hook is down roughly thirteen points. It still clears the 30 percent floor in ad-metrics-glossary.md, but hold sits under the 12 to 15 percent floor in both windows.

The honest caveat on all of it: nobody has told this brain what winning looks like. The Parker brand context document states the brand's success definition as "Consistent 2x ROAS at $1K adspent" with "ROAS is our main KPI," and that is a stated claim, not a verified target. The account is running $11,491.04 a day, roughly eleven times that stated spend level, at a 1.33 ROAS. Either the stated target is stale, or it means $1,000 a day, or the account is deliberately buying growth below the stated bar. The brand intake was never run, so there is no spend-versus-efficiency tiebreak and no unit economics to settle it. Until Hoolest answers, every efficiency judgment in this doc is measured against general D2C benchmarks rather than the brand's own bar, and should be read that way.

The split is not a targeting decision, it is the auction re-sorting itself around new creative, and the cleanest evidence is that the two formats disagree with each other. So the account is not converging on one buyer, it is running two different books at once: a video book that skews female and an image book that skews male. Neither is wrong, but the aggregate 49.6 to 49.2 number hides both of them, and a reader who only saw the account line would conclude the audience is neutral when in fact it is split by format.

Individual ads swing much harder than either aggregate, which matters for anyone reading a single ad's numbers. That is a thing to watch, not yet a problem to fix.

It is worth ruling out the obvious seasonal explanation, because `seasonality.md` says a gifting window can flip a brand's whole buyer profile, and it cites a case where a brand's buyers went from predominantly male to an even split during a holiday. VeRelief is a giftable product and Father's Day fell on 2026-06-21, inside this window. But the monthly cuts do not support that read. The most female-heavy month was July at 52.5 percent female against 46.3 percent male, and the only holiday on the brand's list that falls in July is July 4th, which is a patriotic moment rather than a person-to-person gifting occasion. The Father's Day cut, June 10 to 30, was actually the most male-heavy of the four at 51.2 percent male, the opposite of what a gifting flip would produce. Verified from the four windowed pulls. So the flip tracks the creative and the platform, not the calendar. And nothing in the brand context document claims a target gender, so there is no stated intent to measure any of this drift against. Data-limited on intent, verified on the numbers.

## Baseline account metrics, last 90 days

The account's headline numbers moved in a genuinely good direction quarter over quarter. ROAS went 1.24 to 1.33. CPA went $217.71 to $151.11, a 30.6 percent improvement. Cost per add to cart went from $61.46 to $30.83, and cost per checkout initiated from $190.48 to $69.92, both computed from the returned totals. Cost per lead went $101.15 to $88.64. CPM rose modestly from $30.58 to $32.50, up 6.3 percent, which is a mild cost-of-media increase, not an alarm. Verified from the two pulls. Read the way `ad-account-analysis.md` frames profitability, the account got a sustainable amount of spend behind a doubled purchase count at a materially lower cost per purchase. That is the shape of a winning quarter.

Two headline numbers look worse and only one of them is real. AOV fell 25.4 percent from $269.95 to $201.29, and clicks-all CTR fell from 5.62 percent to 2.23 percent. The AOV drop is real and it is a product-mix story: the custom conversion totals show Hoolest Pro purchases falling from 267 to 31, Stress Protection Plan falling from 447 to zero, and Mini Max PEMF from 43 to 13, while VeRelief Prime purchases rose from 2,268 to 5,141. The account converged on the $159 device and the average order came down with it. Verified from list_custom_metrics totals across both windows. The CTR drop is mostly an artifact. Last window, four ads carrying just $52,256.12 of spend, 7.1 percent of the total, generated 707,968 clicks, which is 52.4 percent of every click in the account. Those were the Flex PEMF videos and the Dr. Jenifer UGC ad, running at cost per click figures of $0.02, $0.12, $0.15 and $0.16, and together they delivered 136 purchases against 3,371. Verified. Strip that cheap-click creative out and the CTR comparison stops meaning what it appears to mean. The more honest read is link-click CTR, which went 2.53 percent to 1.11 percent, still down but now just above the 1 percent starting-point benchmark rather than off a cliff.

The two metrics genuinely worth watching into next quarter are the within-quarter trend and hook rate. Broken into calendar cuts that sum exactly to the quarter total, the account ran $151,693.90 across June 10 to 30 at a 1.44 ROAS, $137.65 CPA and $32.39 CPM with 149 ads live; $416,856.73 in July at 1.38, $138.22 and $29.59 with 360 ads live; $400,065.09 in August at 1.27, $171.70 and $34.36 with 672 ads live; and $77,069.20 across September 1 to 8 at 1.15, $166.10 and $43.81 with 227 ads live. Hold that decline against the calendar and the calendar does not explain it. Two ads, both among the account's five biggest video spenders, separated by a 26-point hook gap and a 23-point hold gap. Verified per ad.

Judged as a growth quarter it worked. Judged against the stated 2x it did not. Until the brand answers the intake, both readings stand and neither can be called the right one. Data-limited.

## Open loops

Pull: **tension.** Two sources cannot both be true as stated. The brand's own written bar and its own account are describing businesses eleven times apart in size, and the audit cannot tell which is the current truth.
Question: What does Hoolest treat as a good ROAS, CPA and spend level today?
Why it is a loop: Every efficiency call in this audit and in every downstream doc is currently graded against generic D2C benchmarks. With a real bar, a 1.33 blended ROAS is either a good growth quarter or an unprofitable one, and the same account read produces opposite recommendations.
Territory: Product. **Only the brand can answer this. Routes to Hoolest.**
